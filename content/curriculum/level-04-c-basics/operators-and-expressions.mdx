In Foundations you evaluated expressions by precedence, one operator at a time. C has more operators and one big new rule: what an operator does depends on the types of its operands.

## Integer division throws away the fraction

When both operands of `/` are integers, C does integer division: the result is an integer, with the fraction thrown away (rounded toward zero). `%` gives the remainder. Predict all six lines.

<CRun program="c-basics/division" caption='Dividing ints and doubles' predict />

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

- `7 / 2` is `3` and `7 % 2` is `1`: 7 = 2 × 3 + 1.
- `-7 / 2` is `-3`, not -4: C rounds toward zero. And `-7 % 2` is `-1`, because C keeps `(a / b) * b + a % b == a`. So `n % 2 == 1` is not a safe test for odd numbers when `n` can be negative; use `n % 2 != 0`.
- `7 / 2.0` is `3.500000`: one `double` operand makes it a `double` division.
- `(double)7 / 2` is also `3.5`. `(double)` is a **cast**: it converts 7 to a `double` before the division.

</details>

The classic trap is an average:

<CRun program="c-basics/average-bug" caption='A lost fraction' />

`total / count` is worked out first, as ints, giving 4. Only then is it stored in a `double`. Converting afterwards can't bring the fraction back. Write `(double)total / count` instead, and it prints `4.250000`.

## Precedence and order

C applies `*`, `/` and `%` before `+` and `-`, and operators of the same rank go left to right. Brackets override both. Predict the four numbers.

<CRun program="c-basics/precedence" caption='Which operator goes first?' predict />

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

`14 20 10 2`. `2 + 3 * 4` multiplies first: 14. Brackets make `(2 + 3) * 4` 20. `20 - 6 - 4` goes left to right: (20 - 6) - 4 = 10. `2 * 3 % 4`: `*` and `%` share a rank, so left to right, (2 × 3) % 4 = 2.

When you're not sure, add brackets. They cost nothing and tell the next reader what you meant.

</details>

## Comparisons and logic give 0 or 1

C89 had no true and false: a comparison gives the `int` 1 when it holds and 0 when it doesn't, and C still works that way (`<stdbool.h>` adds the names `true` and `false` for 1 and 0).

<CRun program="c-basics/truth-values" caption='What a comparison is worth' />

`&&` (and) is 1 when both sides are nonzero, `||` (or) when either is, and `!` turns nonzero into 0 and 0 into 1. `&&` and `||` also **short-circuit**: if the left side already decides the answer, the right side isn't evaluated at all. That's why `count != 0 && total / count > 2` is safe: when `count` is 0, the division never happens.

Watch the difference between `==` (compare) and `=` (assign). `if (x = 5)` stores 5 in x and is always true; the next lesson shows gcc warning about it.

## Increment and compound assignment

`i++` and `++i` both add 1 to `i`. The difference is the value the expression gives: `i++` gives the old value, `++i` the new one. `total += 5` is short for `total = total + 5`, and `-=`, `*=`, `/=` work the same way.

<CRun program="c-basics/increment" caption='Before or after the increment?' predict />

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

`a = 5, b = 7, i = 7`, then `total = 29`. `a = i++` copies 5 and then makes i 6. `b = ++i` makes i 7 first and copies 7. For total: 10 + 5 = 15, × 2 = 30, - 1 = 29.

Keep `++` on a line of its own, or in a `for` header. Using the same variable twice in one expression with `++`, such as `i = i++ + 1`, is undefined behaviour in C.

</details>

<KeyIdea>An operator's result depends on its operands' types: two ints divide as ints. Comparisons and logic give 1 or 0, and brackets beat any precedence rule you half remember.</KeyIdea>
