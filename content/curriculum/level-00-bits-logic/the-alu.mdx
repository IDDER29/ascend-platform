You now have all the parts of a calculator built from gates: adders, a way to subtract, comparisons and flags, and multiplexers to choose between signals. The <b>ALU</b>, the arithmetic logic unit, puts them together into one circuit that can do several operations and is told which one to do.

By the end you'll be able to explain how an ALU picks an operation, build one slice of it, and describe how slices combine into an 8-bit ALU.

## Many answers at once, one chosen

The trick is simple, and it may feel wasteful at first: the ALU works out every answer at the same time. The adder, the AND gates and the OR gates all get A and B and all produce their results. Then a multiplexer lets just one of them through. The multiplexer's select wires are the <b>operation code</b>, a few bits that say which answer you want.

Here is one possible design for a 4-bit ALU with four operations, chosen by a 2-bit code, with A = 0110 (6) and B = 0011 (3):

<KeyIdea>An ALU computes every result in parallel and uses the operation code to choose which one comes out.</KeyIdea>

## One bit at a time

An ALU is built from identical <b>slices</b>, one per bit. Each slice takes one bit of A, one bit of B, the carry from the slice to its right, and the operation code. It gives out one bit of the result and a carry for the slice to its left. Inside a slice is a full adder, a few logic gates, and a small multiplexer.

Build the logic part of a slice: with select S = 0 the output R should be A AND B, and with S = 1 it should be A OR B. The starting line always gives A AND B, so the S = 1 rows are wrong. Use the multiplexer pattern: (NOT S AND first choice) OR (S AND second choice).

<GateLab caption='A 1-bit ALU slice: AND or OR' inputsJson='["S","A","B"]' outputsJson='["R"]' targetJson='{"R":"(NOT S AND A AND B) OR (S AND (A OR B))"}' startJson='["R = A AND B"]' />

Adding more operations means adding more inputs to the multiplexer and more select bits, not changing the idea.

## Eight slices make an 8-bit ALU

Put eight slices side by side and connect each slice's carry out to the next slice's carry in, exactly like the ripple-carry adder. Now A and B are bytes, and so is the result.

Subtraction needs one more control wire. When it is on, every slice's B input goes through an XOR with that wire (which flips B exactly when the wire is 1), and the carry into the first slice is set to 1. That is "NOT B, plus 1", so the same eight adders now compute A - B.

## Flags come out too

Alongside its result, the ALU produces the flags from the last lesson:

- the <b>zero flag</b>, from a NOR of all eight result bits;
- the <b>carry flag</b>, the carry out of the leftmost slice.

The tiny CPU you'll build later in this path has the smallest useful ALU: it only adds and subtracts, as Ben Eater's breadboard computer does, and every ADD or SUB sets its zero and carry flags. Real CPUs add AND, OR, XOR, shifts and more, but each is just another result the multiplexer can choose.

<KeyIdea>An ALU is slices of adder and logic, chained by carries, with a multiplexer picking the operation and flags describing the result.</KeyIdea>
