A phone has billions of transistors, an operating system of millions of lines of code, and apps on top. No one designs, or understands, all of that at once. People manage it the same way you manage a car: you use the steering wheel and pedals and let the engine be someone else's problem.

By the end you'll be able to explain what abstraction and interfaces are, name the layers of a computer this path climbs, and say why the hidden layers still matter.

## Too much to hold at once

To send a message on your phone, you tap a button. Underneath, the app calls the operating system, which talks to the network hardware, which is built from circuits, which are built from switches flipping between two states. If you had to think about all of it to send a message, you never would.

<b>Abstraction</b> means using something by what it does, without dealing with how it does it. Each layer of a computer is an abstraction over the one below.

## Parts with interfaces

What makes abstraction work is an <b>interface</b>: the promise a part makes to the parts that use it. A car's interface is the wheel, the pedals and what they do. Petrol, electric or hybrid, the interface stays the same, so you can drive any of them.

In a computer, a circuit that adds promises "two numbers in, their sum out". A memory promises "give me an address, I'll give you what's stored there". As long as each part keeps its promise, the parts above can rely on it, and the part itself can be rebuilt faster or cheaper without breaking anything.

<KeyIdea>An interface is a promise. Keep the promise and nobody above needs to know how you keep it.</KeyIdea>

## When a hidden detail shows through

Abstractions hide details; they don't remove them. Sometimes a detail from below shows through:

- Add 1 to a large number and get a negative one: that is how numbers are stored as bits.
- A program crashes only on a certain input: that is how memory is laid out.
- A loop is slow for no visible reason: that is how the CPU reaches memory.

Each of these makes no sense from the top layer alone, and complete sense once you know the layer below.

<KeyIdea>Work at one layer at a time, but know the layers below. That's where the surprises come from.</KeyIdea>
