A queue hands things out in the order they arrived. Often you want something else: the **most urgent** item next, whenever it arrived. That's a **priority queue**, and the usual way to build one is a **binary heap**: a tree kept in a plain array, which gives you the smallest item in O(1) and adds or removes items in O(log n).

## The heap property

A **min-heap** is a binary tree with one rule: every parent is less than or equal to its children. Nothing is said about left versus right, so a heap is much looser than a binary search tree. But the rule is enough to put the smallest item at the root, where it's always ready.

The tree is also **complete**: every row is full except perhaps the last, which fills from the left. That shape is what lets it live in an array.

## A tree in an array

Number the nodes row by row, starting at 0 for the root, and store node i at `heap[i]`. Then, for any index i:

- its children are at `2*i + 1` and `2*i + 2`;
- its parent is at `(i - 1) / 2`, with C's integer division.

No pointers, no allocation per node, and every node sits next to its neighbours in memory.

| index | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| value | 3 | 8 | 12 | 42 | 25 | 31 | 17 |

Here 3 is the root; its children (indexes 1 and 2) are 8 and 12; 8's children (3 and 4) are 42 and 25; 12's (5 and 6) are 31 and 17. Every parent is smaller than its children.

## Adding: sift up

To add a value, put it in the first free slot at the end, which keeps the tree complete. That may break the rule with its parent, so **sift up**: while the new value is smaller than its parent, swap them. It climbs at most one row per swap.

## Taking the top: sift down

The smallest value is `heap[0]`. To remove it, move the **last** value into the root, shrink the heap by one, then **sift down**: while a child is smaller, swap with the **smaller** of the two children. It descends at most one row per swap.

A tree of n items has about log2 n rows, so both operations cost **O(log n)**. Reading the top without removing it is O(1).

"fix the outage" (priority 1) comes first. Then the fire alarm (priority 0), pushed later, jumps straight to the front of what's left: arrival time doesn't matter, only priority. Then priorities 2, 3 and 4.

This is how an operating system can pick the next timer to fire, and how path-finding algorithms such as Dijkstra's pick the closest unvisited place. If two tasks share a priority, a binary heap doesn't promise which comes first, so add a tie-breaker (an arrival counter) when that matters.

The sift down only ever looks at the **left** child (`2*i + 1`). If the right child is the smaller one, it's never compared, so a larger value can stay above it and the heap property breaks. After a few pops, 17 comes out before 12, and 31 before 12.

The fix is the one in the first program: compare both children, pick the smaller, and swap with that one, while either child is smaller than the parent. Swapping with the smaller child also matters: swap with the larger and the new parent would be bigger than its other child.

## Heapsort, in one sentence

Push all n items into a heap, then pop them all: they come out sorted, in O(n log n) total. **Heapsort** does the same inside the array being sorted, using a max-heap and no extra memory. It's never worse than O(n log n), but in practice it's usually slower than a good quicksort: its jumps between parents and children far apart in the array are hard on the cache, as the cache-friendly layout lesson shows.
