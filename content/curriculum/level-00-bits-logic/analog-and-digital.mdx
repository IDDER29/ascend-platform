The real world is smooth. A temperature can be 21.4 degrees or 21.43 or anything in between. Sound is a pressure that rises and falls continuously. Computers don't work that way, and the reason they don't is one of the most important ideas in this whole path.

By the end you'll be able to explain the difference between analog and digital, how a circuit reads a messy voltage as a clean value, and why that makes computers reliable.

## Smooth or in steps

An <b>analog</b> quantity can take any value in a range. The hands of a clock sweep smoothly; the needle on an old speedometer can point anywhere between 0 and 200.

A <b>digital</b> quantity takes only a few separate values, with nothing in between. A digital clock jumps from 10:41 to 10:42. A light switch is on or off.

## A range counts as one value

Inside a computer, information is carried by voltages on wires. A wire's voltage is analog: it is never exactly the same twice, because of heat, interference and the parts themselves.

So a digital circuit makes an agreement. Any voltage in a <b>low band</b> counts as 0. Any voltage in a <b>high band</b> counts as 1. Between the bands is a gap that a correct circuit never stays in. For a chip running at 3.3 volts, roughly 0 to 0.8 volts might count as 0 and 2 to 3.3 volts as 1.

That is the <b>digital abstraction</b>: we pretend the wire holds a clean 0 or 1, and the pretence is safe because of the bands.

<KeyIdea>Digital does not mean perfect voltages. It means a whole range of messy voltages is read as one clean value.</KeyIdea>

## Why computers go digital

The bands are what make digital machines reliable.

- <b>Noise</b>: a little interference nudges a voltage, but it stays in its band, so the value read is unchanged. An analog signal just carries the noise along.
- <b>Copying</b>: every analog copy adds a little noise, so a copy of a copy gets worse. A digital copy is read back as the same values, so the millionth copy is as good as the first.
- <b>Building big</b>: each part only has to land in the right band, not hit an exact value, so billions of slightly different transistors can work together.

The cost is that a digital value can't hold "a bit more than 0". To store something smooth, like sound, a computer measures it many times a second and writes each measurement down as a number. You'll see how in Binary & Data.

<KeyIdea>Clean values in, clean values out: reading ranges as values is what lets digital machines ignore noise and copy perfectly.</KeyIdea>
