In the last lesson you saw that a pattern of bits means nothing until two sides agree on a code. In this project you make a code yourself, use it, and break it. There's nothing to install: a pencil and paper are enough.

For each part, work out your answer first, then open "Check your answer".

## Part 1: How many bits?

You and a friend want to send each other the weather in as few bits as possible. There are four kinds of weather: sun, rain, snow and wind.

Each extra bit doubles the number of patterns you can make:

<TruthTable caption='Bits and patterns' rowsJson='[["bits", "patterns", "how many"], ["1", "0, 1", "2"], ["2", "00, 01, 10, 11", "4"], ["3", "000 to 111", "8"], ["4", "0000 to 1111", "16"]]' />

How many bits do you need for four kinds of weather? And if you add fog and hail, so there are six?

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

Four messages need 2 bits, because 2 bits make exactly 4 patterns. Six messages need 3 bits. Two bits give only 4 patterns, which is too few, and 3 bits give 8, so two patterns are left over.

</details>

## Part 2: Encode and decode

Here is a codebook for the six kinds of weather:

<TruthTable caption='Codebook A: the weather' rowsJson='[["pattern", "message"], ["000", "sun"], ["001", "rain"], ["010", "snow"], ["011", "wind"], ["100", "fog"], ["101", "hail"]]' />

Encode this forecast for three days: rain, rain, sun.

Then decode this one, which your friend sent: `011 100 000`.

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

Rain, rain, sun is `001 001 000`. Your friend's forecast is wind, fog, sun.

Notice that the receiver can split the bits into messages because every pattern is exactly 3 bits long. Morse code uses patterns of different lengths, so it needs short gaps between letters instead.

</details>

## Part 3: Same bits, different book

Your friend has another codebook for a game, also with 3 bits:

<TruthTable caption='Codebook B: a game' rowsJson='[["pattern", "message"], ["000", "start"], ["001", "stop"], ["010", "left"], ["011", "right"], ["100", "jump"], ["101", "pause"]]' />

You send `011 100 000`, meaning wind, fog, sun. Your friend reads it with codebook B. What do they think you said?

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

Right, jump, start. The bits arrived perfectly; not one of them changed. The message still went wrong, because the meaning was never in the bits. It was in the codebook, and the two of you used different ones.

</details>

<KeyIdea>Bits get from one place to another. Meaning only gets there if both sides use the same codebook.</KeyIdea>

## Part 4: Fix the broken codebook

Someone made this codebook for five messages, and it doesn't work:

<TruthTable caption='A broken codebook' rowsJson='[["pattern", "message"], ["00", "yes"], ["01", "no"], ["10", "maybe"], ["11", "later"], ["11", "never"]]' />

What's wrong, and what is the smallest fix?

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

"Later" and "never" share the pattern 11, so a receiver who sees 11 can't tell which one was meant. Five messages don't fit in 2 bits, which only make 4 patterns. The fix is 3 bits: for example 000 yes, 001 no, 010 maybe, 011 later, 100 never.

</details>

## Done when

You can say how many bits a set of messages needs, encode and decode with a codebook, and explain in one sentence why the same bits can mean two different things. Then do the assignment: make a codebook you'd really use.
