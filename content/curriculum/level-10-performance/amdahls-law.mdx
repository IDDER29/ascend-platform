You've made one part of a program much faster. How much faster is the whole program? Usually much less than you hoped, and Amdahl's law says exactly how much less.

## The law

Say a fraction **p** of a program's time is spent in a part you speed up by a factor **s**, and the rest, **1 - p**, stays as it was. Measured against the old time (call it 1), the new time is the untouched part plus the improved part:

**new time = (1 - p) + p ÷ s**

so the overall speedup is

**speedup = 1 ÷ ((1 - p) + p ÷ s)**

80% of a program's time is in one function. You make that function 4 times faster. What is the overall speedup?

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

1 ÷ ((1 - 0.8) + 0.8 ÷ 4) = 1 ÷ (0.2 + 0.2) = 1 ÷ 0.4 = **2.5**.

A 4-times faster function gives a 2.5-times faster program. The 20% you didn't touch now takes as long as the whole improved 80%.

</details>

## The limit

Make **s** as large as you like: the improved part's time `p ÷ s` shrinks towards 0, but the untouched part stays. The speedup can never exceed

**1 ÷ (1 - p)**

For p = 0.8 that's 5, however fast the function becomes. For p = 0.9 it's 10, and even p = 0.99 caps the speedup at 100.

A program is 90% parallel: that part can be split between processors. On 16 cores, what speedup does Amdahl's law predict, and what's the limit with unlimited cores?

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

On 16 cores: 1 ÷ (0.1 + 0.9 ÷ 16) = 1 ÷ (0.1 + 0.05625) = 1 ÷ 0.15625 = **6.4**. The limit is 1 ÷ 0.1 = **10**. 16 cores give 6.4 times, not 16: each core added is worth less than the one before.

</details>

<KeyIdea>Speedup = 1 ÷ ((1 - p) + p ÷ s), and it can never exceed 1 ÷ (1 - p). The part you didn't improve sets the limit.</KeyIdea>

## Choosing what to improve

A program spends 30% of its time waiting for storage and 70% computing. You can afford one of two upgrades:

- storage 3 times faster, or
- computing 1.5 times faster.

Which gives the bigger overall speedup?

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

- Storage: 1 ÷ (0.7 + 0.3 ÷ 3) = 1 ÷ (0.7 + 0.1) = 1 ÷ 0.8 = **1.25**.
- Computing: 1 ÷ (0.3 + 0.7 ÷ 1.5) = 1 ÷ (0.3 + 0.467) ≈ 1 ÷ 0.767 ≈ **1.30**.

The modest improvement to the big part wins over the large improvement to the small part. That's the practical lesson of Amdahl's law: make the common case fast, and measure where the time goes before choosing.

</details>

The law isn't wrong; its assumptions don't hold on this machine:

- **The cores aren't identical.** This laptop has 2 performance cores, each running two hardware threads that share it, and 8 slower efficiency cores. The work was split evenly, so the parallel part finished only when the slowest thread did.
- **Busy cores run slower.** With more cores working, the chip lowers its clock speed to stay within its power limit (the subject of a later lesson).

Your numbers will differ: they vary by run, by machine and by what else is running. On a machine with many identical cores, the curve follows Amdahl much more closely.

## A different question: Gustafson's law

Amdahl's law keeps the **problem size fixed** and asks how much faster it gets. Often, given more processors, people solve a **bigger problem** in the same time instead: a finer simulation or a larger image. If the parallel part grows with the number of processors N while the serial part stays the same, the speedup is **(1 - p) + p × N**, where p is the parallel share measured on the parallel machine. For p = 0.9 and N = 16 that's 0.1 + 14.4 = 14.5. This is **Gustafson's law**: not a contradiction of Amdahl, but an answer to a different question.
