The last two lessons looked at one cache. A real CPU has three, sometimes four, stacked between the core and DRAM. This lesson is about why, and about how to put a number on the result.

## Why a hierarchy

There is no memory that is big, fast and cheap at once. A larger cache needs longer wires and more comparisons, so it's slower, and it costs more area and power. So designers stack them:

- **L1:** tiny (32 to 48 KiB of data per core), 4 to 5 cycles, separate for instructions and data, private to a core.
- **L2:** bigger (1 to 2 MiB here), around 12 to 15 cycles, usually private to a core or a small cluster.
- **L3:** many MiB, 40 cycles or more, shared by every core.
- **DRAM:** gigabytes, hundreds of cycles.

Locality means most accesses hit near the top, so the average stays close to the L1's speed while the capacity is the L3's.

## Average memory access time

The standard way to put one number on a hierarchy is the **average memory access time**:

```
AMAT = hit time + miss rate × miss penalty
```

The miss penalty is itself the AMAT of the next level down, so the formula nests. Here is a worked example in cycles:

| Level | Hit time | Miss rate (of accesses that reach it) |
|---|---|---|
| L1 | 4 | 5% |
| L2 | 12 | 20% |
| L3 | 40 | 30% |
| DRAM | 200 | none |

- **L1 alone, straight to DRAM:** 4 + 0.05 × 200 = **14 cycles**
- **With the L2:** 4 + 0.05 × (12 + 0.20 × 200) = 4 + 0.05 × 52 = **6.6 cycles**
- **With L2 and L3:** 4 + 0.05 × (12 + 0.20 × (40 + 0.30 × 200)) = 4 + 0.05 × (12 + 0.20 × 100) = 4 + 0.05 × 32 = **5.6 cycles**

Two questions:

1. What fraction of all accesses go to DRAM?
2. A designer can make the L1 bigger, cutting its miss rate to 4%, but its hit time rises to 5 cycles. With the three-level numbers above, is that worth it?

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

1. 0.05 × 0.20 × 0.30 = 0.003: **0.3%** of accesses. The 20% and 30% are **local** miss rates, out of the accesses that reach that level. The 0.3% is the **global** miss rate of the L3, out of all accesses. Quote the wrong one and an L3 looks either useless or perfect.
2. No. The new AMAT is 5 + 0.04 × 32 = 6.28 cycles, worse than 5.6. Every access pays the L1 hit time, but only 5% of accesses benefited from the lower miss rate. This is why L1 caches stay small: the hit time matters more than the last percent of hits.

</details>

<KeyIdea>AMAT = hit time + miss rate × miss penalty, nested level by level. Small, fast caches near the core and big ones further away give an average close to the fastest and a capacity close to the biggest.</KeyIdea>

## Inclusive, exclusive or neither

When a line is in the L1, is it also in the L2 and L3?

- **Inclusive:** yes, always. A core checking whether another core has a line only needs to look in the shared L3, which simplifies coherence. The cost: the same data takes up space twice, and evicting a line from the L3 forces it out of every L1 and L2 too.
- **Exclusive:** a line lives in exactly one level, and moves between them. The total capacity is the sum of the levels, at the cost of more moving of lines.
- **Non-inclusive (neither):** no guarantee either way. Many recent Intel and AMD designs have a non-inclusive L3 with a separate directory to track which core holds what.

On the laptop above, the L2 caches total 6.5 MiB against a 12 MiB L3, and each L2 holds 1.25 or 2 MiB. Why would a strictly inclusive L3 be a poor fit here?

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

An inclusive L3 must hold a copy of everything in every L2. With 6.5 MiB of L2 in total, over half of the 12 MiB L3 could be taken up by duplicates, leaving much less room for anything else. As private L2s grew, designers moved away from strict inclusion for exactly this reason.

</details>
