The Byte Lab shows bytes and reads them several ways at once: as unsigned and signed numbers, as hex, as ASCII and UTF-8 text, as a colour, and as a 16-bit number. In this project you use it to investigate. For each part, predict first, then try it in the Lab, then open "Check your answer".

## Part 1: One byte, five readings

Set the first byte to 65. Predict what each reading will show: unsigned, signed, hex, binary and ASCII. Then set it to 255 and predict again.

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

65 is 65 unsigned and signed, 0x41, 0100 0001, and the letter 'A'. 255 is 255 unsigned but -1 signed, 0xFF, 1111 1111, and no printable character at all.

The bits are the only thing stored. The five readings are five codebooks applied to them.

</details>

## Part 2: The case bit

With the byte at 65 ('A'), flip just the bit worth 32. What letter do you get? Now try the same with 90 ('Z'). Then use the "Try" row: XOR with 32 (type 0x20), and press "Keep it".

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

65 becomes 97, 'a', and 90 becomes 122, 'z'. ASCII was designed so that every lowercase letter is its capital plus 32. That makes the bit worth 32 a case switch: XOR 32 flips the case, AND with 1101 1111 (0xDF) forces a capital, and OR with 32 forces lowercase.

</details>

## Part 3: Read a setting with a mask

A program keeps eight on/off settings in one byte, one per bit. The byte is 0010 1101 (45). Setting number 2 (the bit worth 4) means "dark mode". Is dark mode on? Use AND with a mask to find out, then switch dark mode off without touching any other setting.

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

45 AND 4 (0000 0100) is 4, not 0, so dark mode is on. To switch it off and keep everything else, AND with the opposite mask, 1111 1011 (0xFB): 45 AND 251 is 41, 0010 1001. Only the bit worth 4 changed.

</details>

## Part 4: Which byte comes first?

Use the plus button to add a second byte. Set the two bytes to 0x34 and 0x12. Look at the 16-bit reading: it shows two numbers. Which is which, and which one is right?

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

Low byte first (little-endian, as on x86 and most ARM machines), 34 12 is 0x1234, which is 4660. High byte first (big-endian, as in many network protocols), it's 0x3412, which is 13330. Neither is "right": the file format or the machine decides the order, and reading with the wrong one gives a wildly wrong number.

</details>

## Part 5: The mystery message

Someone sends you three bytes: 72, 105, 33. Set them in the Lab. Read them as numbers, as text and as a colour. What did they mean?

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

As numbers they're 72, 105 and 33. As text they say "Hi!". As a colour they're #486921, a dark olive green. All three readings are valid, and nothing in the bytes says which was meant. "Hi!" looks likely, but if the sender was a drawing app, it's a colour. You can only be sure once you know the format, which is the codebook lesson again.

</details>

<KeyIdea>Bytes don't say what they are. To read them right, you need to know the format they were written in.</KeyIdea>

## Done when

You've made all five predictions and checked each in the Lab, and you can explain, for any byte you're shown, why it might be a number, a letter or part of a colour. Then do the assignment: read the first bytes of a real PNG file.
