A chip turns electrical energy into computation, and into heat. How much it can do is limited less by how many transistors fit than by how much power it can draw and how much heat it can shed. This lesson puts numbers on that.

## Power and energy

- **Power** is a rate, measured in **watts**: joules per second. It decides heat, the battery's drain rate, and how big the cooler must be.
- **Energy** is a total, measured in **joules**: power × time. It decides how much battery a task uses, and the electricity bill.

A processor drawing 10 W for 3 seconds uses 30 J. Another drawing 6 W for 4 seconds uses 24 J: it uses less energy despite running longer. Low power and low energy are different goals.

## Where the power goes

Most of a chip's power is **dynamic**: every time a transistor switches, it charges or drains a tiny capacitance. Summed over the chip:

**dynamic power ≈ α × C × V² × f**

where:
- **α** is how much of the chip switches each cycle;
- **C** is the capacitance being switched;
- **V** is the supply voltage;
- **f** is the clock rate.

On top of that there's **static** (leakage) power: transistors leak current even when they're not switching, as long as they're powered.

The voltage is squared. A faster clock usually needs a higher voltage too, so pushing f up costs power faster than linearly. A chip lowers its voltage and its clock rate both by 15%. What fraction of its dynamic power is left? And for a task with a fixed amount of work, what fraction of the dynamic **energy**?

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

- **Power:** 0.85² × 0.85 = 0.85³ ≈ **0.614**, so about 39% less power for 15% less speed.
- **Energy:** the task now takes 1 ÷ 0.85 ≈ 1.18 times as long, so energy = 0.614 × 1.18 ≈ 0.85² ≈ **0.72**, about 28% less. With the clock divided out, dynamic energy per operation goes with V².

That's why chips run slower and at lower voltage when they can, and why efficiency cores exist: a core designed for lower voltage and frequency does the same work for less energy.

</details>

## Race to idle, or slow and steady?

A task must finish within 2 seconds. The chip draws **3 W of static power** whenever it's awake, plus **9 W dynamic** at full speed, where the task takes 1 second. Asleep, it draws 0.2 W. Compare the energy of three strategies over the 2 seconds:

1. **Race to idle:** full speed for 1 s, then sleep for 1 s.
2. **Half the clock, same voltage:** 2 s awake.
3. **Half the clock, 80% of the voltage:** 2 s awake.

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

1. **Race to idle:** (3 + 9) × 1 + 0.2 × 1 = **12.2 J**.
2. **Half the clock, same voltage:** dynamic power halves to 4.5 W, but the chip is awake twice as long: (3 + 4.5) × 2 = **15 J**. Worse: the static power was paid for twice as long, and the dynamic energy didn't change at all.
3. **Half the clock, 80% voltage:** dynamic is 9 × 0.5 × 0.8² = 2.88 W, so (3 + 2.88) × 2 = **11.76 J**. Slightly better than racing.

There's no single answer. Slowing the clock alone wastes energy, because static power keeps running; racing to idle wins when sleep is cheap and leakage is high. Slowing down wins only if voltage can come down with the clock. Modern chips and operating systems make this choice constantly.

</details>

<KeyIdea>Dynamic power goes with V² × f; energy is power × time. Lowering voltage is the big lever, static power punishes running longer, and the best strategy depends on both.</KeyIdea>

## The end of Dennard scaling

For decades, each new manufacturing process shrank transistors. Under **Dennard scaling** (1974), shrinking a transistor also lowered its voltage and capacitance in proportion, so **power per area stayed constant** while clocks rose and transistor counts doubled. Each generation was faster at the same heat.

Around 2005 it broke. Voltage couldn't keep falling: near a transistor's threshold voltage, leakage grows sharply and switching becomes unreliable. With voltage stuck, a faster clock meant more power per area than a chip could get rid of as heat. Clock rates stopped near 3 to 5 GHz, where they still are.

Transistor counts kept growing, so designers spent them differently:

- **more cores** instead of faster ones (Amdahl's law now matters to everyone);
- **specialised units** that do one job efficiently;
- turning parts of the chip **off** when idle.

Today a chip has more transistors than it can power at full speed at once, so at any moment much of it must stay off or slowed. That unused fraction is called **dark silicon**, and it's why modern chips mix big cores, efficiency cores, GPUs and accelerators, each switched on only when its kind of work arrives.
