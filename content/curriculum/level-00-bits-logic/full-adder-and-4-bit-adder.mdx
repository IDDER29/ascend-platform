The half-adder added two single bits. Real numbers have many bits, and adding them column by column means carrying, just like adding on paper. This lesson adds the missing piece, the <b>carry in</b>, and then chains four adders together to add two 4-bit numbers.

By the end you'll be able to read a full adder's table, trace a 4-bit addition with its carries, and explain how the same circuit subtracts.

## The half-adder's missing input

Add 0110 and 0111 by hand, right to left. The first column is 0 + 1: two bits, a half-adder can do that. The second column is 1 + 1 = 0, carry 1. Now the third column has <i>three</i> bits to add: 1, 1 and the carry coming in from the right.

A half-adder has only two inputs. Every column except the first needs a circuit with three.

## The full adder

A <b>full adder</b> takes three bits, A, B and the <b>carry in</b>, and produces two: the <b>sum</b> bit for this column and the <b>carry out</b> for the next one. Inside it's two half-adders and an OR gate: the first adds A and B, the second adds the carry in to that result, and the OR combines their two carries.

Read it as counting: the outputs are just how many 1s came in, written in binary. Three 1s in gives 11: sum 1, carry 1.

<KeyIdea>A full adder adds three bits, the two digits and the carry from the column to its right, and passes its own carry on.</KeyIdea>

## Four in a row: the ripple-carry adder

Line up four full adders, one per column. Feed each one its pair of bits. Connect each adder's carry out to the next adder's carry in, and give the rightmost one a carry in of 0. That's a 4-bit adder.

Each has inputs A and B from above and a sum output below. An arrow carries the carry out of each box into the carry in of the box to its left. The rightmost box has carry in 0; the leftmost box&#39;s carry out leaves the circuit.' />

Trace 0110 + 0111 (6 + 7). Bit 0: 0 + 1 = 1. Bit 1: 1 + 1 = 0, carry 1. Bit 2: 1 + 1 + 1 = 1, carry 1. Bit 3: 0 + 0 + 1 = 1. The answer is 1101, which is 13.

The carry has to <i>ripple</i> through every adder in turn, so a wider adder takes longer to settle. Real CPUs use cleverer carry-lookahead circuits to work out the carries faster, but the idea is the same.

## The same adder subtracts

From two's complement you know A - B is A + (-B), and -B is "flip every bit of B, then add 1". The adder does both at once: put NOT B on the B inputs and set the first carry in to 1 instead of 0. That 1 is the "add 1".

So with a few NOT gates and one control wire, the same row of full adders adds or subtracts. That's the core of the <b>ALU</b>, the arithmetic unit at the centre of every processor.

<KeyIdea>To subtract, feed the adder NOT B and a first carry in of 1. One circuit, both operations.</KeyIdea>
