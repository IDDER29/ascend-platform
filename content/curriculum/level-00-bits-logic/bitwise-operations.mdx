You've used AND, OR, XOR and NOT on single bits. A computer can do them to every bit of a byte at once, in one step. This lesson turns those gates into tools: <b>masks</b> to read and change chosen bits, and <b>shifts</b> to move bits around.

By the end you'll be able to work out a bitwise AND, OR or XOR of two bytes, use a mask to read, set, clear or flip any bit, and say what a shift does to a number.

## Gates, on a whole byte at once

A bitwise operation lines two numbers up and applies one gate to each column separately. There are no carries; each column is on its own.

<TruthTable caption='1100 and 1010, column by column' rowsJson='[["operation", "result"], ["1100 AND 1010", "1000"], ["1100 OR 1010", "1110"], ["1100 XOR 1010", "0110"], ["NOT 1100", "0011"]]' />

In C these are written `&` (AND), `|` (OR), `^` (XOR) and `~` (NOT). Watch out: `&&` and `||` are different operators. They treat each whole value as true or false, while `&` and `|` work bit by bit.

## Masks: picking out bits

A <b>mask</b> is a number chosen for its pattern of 1s. Combined with a value, it lets you touch only the bits you care about.

- <b>Read</b> with AND: `x & 0x0F` keeps the low four bits and clears the rest, because anything AND 0 is 0 and anything AND 1 stays itself.
- <b>Set</b> with OR: `x | 0b0100` turns bit 2 on and leaves every other bit as it was.
- <b>Clear</b> with AND and NOT: `x & ~0b0100` turns bit 2 off.
- <b>Flip</b> with XOR: `x ^ 0b0100` flips bit 2.

<KeyIdea>AND keeps, OR sets, XOR flips. The mask says which bits; the rest are left alone.</KeyIdea>

## Shifts: moving bits

A shift slides every bit left or right. `x << 1` moves every bit one place left and puts a 0 in at the right; `x >> 1` moves them right. Since each place is worth twice the one to its right, shifting left by one doubles an unsigned number, and shifting right by one halves it (dropping any remainder). So 5 is 0101, and `5 << 1` is 1010, which is 10.

Shifts also build masks: `1 << n` is a number with only bit n set. That's why the set, clear and flip idioms are usually written `x | (1 << n)`, `x & ~(1 << n)` and `x ^ (1 << n)`.

## Where you'll meet them

Colours are often stored as one number, 0xRRGGBB. To get the green part, shift it down and mask it: `(colour >> 8) & 0xFF`. Unix file permissions, network headers and the CPU's own flags are all packed the same way. Whenever several yes-or-no facts share one number, masks are how you read them.
