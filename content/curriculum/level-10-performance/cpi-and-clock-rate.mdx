"Faster" needs a number. For a processor running one program, the number that matters is how long the program takes. This lesson splits that time into three factors, measures them on real hardware, and shows why improving one can make another worse.

## The CPU time equation

The time a program spends running on the CPU is:

**CPU time = instruction count × CPI × clock period**

- **Instruction count:** how many instructions actually run. It depends on the program, the compiler and the instruction set.
- **CPI, cycles per instruction:** the average number of clock cycles each instruction takes. It depends on the processor's design and on which instructions run.
- **Clock period:** the length of one cycle, the inverse of the clock rate. At 3 GHz a cycle lasts 1 ÷ 3e9 seconds, about 0.33 ns.

Equivalently, CPU time = instructions × CPI ÷ clock rate.

Design A runs a program in 2 billion instructions with an average CPI of 1.5 at 3 GHz. Design B runs the same instructions at 4 GHz, but its deeper pipeline makes the average CPI 2.25. Which finishes first?

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

- A: 2e9 × 1.5 ÷ 3e9 = **1.0 s**.
- B: 2e9 × 2.25 ÷ 4e9 = **1.125 s**.

A is faster even though B's clock is a third faster. Clock rate alone says nothing until you know the CPI. Chip makers once competed on gigahertz until designs like this made the number meaningless.

</details>

## Average CPI from a mix

Different instructions take different numbers of cycles, so CPI is an average weighted by how often each kind runs. A program's instructions are 50% ALU operations (1 cycle each), 30% loads (2 cycles) and 20% branches (3 cycles). What is the average CPI?

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

CPI = 0.5 × 1 + 0.3 × 2 + 0.2 × 3 = 0.5 + 0.6 + 0.6 = **1.7**.

This weighting is why "the CPI" of a processor doesn't exist on its own: change the program's mix, and the average changes.

</details>

## Trade-offs between the factors

A new compiler cuts the program's instruction count by 20%, from 2 billion to 1.6 billion, but the instructions it chooses are slower, raising CPI from 1.5 to 1.8. The clock stays at 3 GHz. Is the program faster, and by how much?

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

New time: 1.6e9 × 1.8 ÷ 3e9 = **0.96 s**, against 1.0 s before. It is faster, but only by 1.0 ÷ 0.96, about **1.04 times**, not the 1.25 times the instruction count suggests. The 20% fewer instructions were mostly eaten by the 20% higher CPI.

</details>

<KeyIdea>CPU time = instructions × CPI × clock period. A design that improves one factor but worsens another can come out ahead, behind or even.</KeyIdea>

## Measuring it for real

This program adds up a 64 KiB array of numbers 5000 times: 82 million additions. It times itself, and also reads the processor's **time stamp counter** (TSC), which ticks at a constant rate. The same program is built twice: at `-O0`, then at `-O2`. Predict how much faster `-O2` will be.

<CRun program="architect/cpi-sum-O0" caption='Summing an array, built with -O0' />

<CRun program="architect/cpi-sum-O2" caption='The same program, built with -O2' predict />

Here are the two loops from `gcc -S`. At `-O0`, each element runs these 11 instructions (the loop body at `.L3`, then the test at `.L2`):

```
.L3:
	movl	-4(%rbp), %eax
	cltq
	leaq	0(,%rax,4), %rdx
	movq	-24(%rbp), %rax
	addq	%rdx, %rax
	movl	(%rax), %eax
	addl	%eax, -8(%rbp)
	addl	$1, -4(%rbp)
.L2:
	movl	-4(%rbp), %eax
	cmpl	-28(%rbp), %eax
	jl	.L3
```

At `-O2`, just 4:

```
.L3:
	addl	(%rdi), %eax
	addq	$4, %rdi
	cmpq	%rdx, %rdi
	jne	.L3
```

Using the measurements, work out the ticks per instruction for each build, and show that the two factors multiply to the speedup.

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

In this run, `-O0` took 8.10 ticks per element and `-O2` took 1.85:

- **Instructions per element:** 11 against 4, so `-O2` runs 2.75 times fewer.
- **Ticks per instruction:** `-O0` is 8.10 ÷ 11 ≈ 0.74, and `-O2` is 1.85 ÷ 4 ≈ 0.46, so each `-O2` instruction is about 1.59 times cheaper.
- **Together:** 2.75 × 1.59 ≈ 4.4, matching the measured times: 0.266 s ÷ 0.061 s ≈ 4.4.

Why is each `-O0` instruction slower? Its sum lives in memory: `addl %eax, -8(%rbp)` reads the sum, adds and writes it back. The next iteration must wait for that store before reading it again. `-O2` keeps the sum in `%eax`, a register, so the additions follow each other far more quickly.

Your numbers will differ: these vary from run to run and machine to machine.

</details>

## Ticks are not quite cycles

The TSC counts at a constant rate, which the program measured as 2.50 GHz. The core's real clock is not constant. This laptop's fast cores can run anywhere from 0.4 GHz to 4.6 GHz, moving many times a second with load and temperature:

```
$ lscpu
...
Model name:                              13th Gen Intel(R) Core(TM) i5-1335U
CPU max MHz:                             4600.0000
CPU min MHz:                             400.0000
...
```

So "ticks per instruction" is CPI measured against a steady reference clock, not against the core's own cycles. Converting needs the core's actual frequency during the run: true CPI = ticks per instruction × (core clock ÷ 2.50 GHz). Tools like `perf stat` read the core's own cycle and instruction counters directly, but they need permission that this machine doesn't grant to ordinary users, which is common on shared and locked-down systems.

What would the `-O2` loop's true CPI be if the core ran at 3.7 GHz during the measurement?

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

0.46 × (3.7 ÷ 2.50) = 0.46 × 1.48 ≈ **0.68 cycles per instruction**: fewer than one, because a modern core runs several instructions in the same cycle. CPI below 1 is normal today, so architects often quote its inverse, **IPC** (instructions per cycle): about 1.5 here.

</details>
