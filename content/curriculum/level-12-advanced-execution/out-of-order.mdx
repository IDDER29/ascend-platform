In the last lesson, an in-order core could only pair instructions that were already in a good order, and you had to schedule them yourself. An **out-of-order** core does that scheduling in hardware while the program runs: it starts each instruction as soon as its inputs are ready, whatever its place in the program.

## Let the ready ones go

This block starts with a load (3 cycles) and an add that needs it. The other four instructions don't depend on the load at all. Run it in order, one per cycle, then switch to out of order. Predict both times first.

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

**In order: 9 cycles.** The load issues in cycle 1 and its result is ready in cycle 4, so `add r3` waits until then. Every instruction behind it waits too, even though they don't need r1: they issue in cycles 5 to 8, and the last commits in 9.

**Out of order: 7 cycles.** `add r4` and `add r6` don't need the load, so they issue in cycles 2 and 3, while the load is still running. `add r3` goes at 4, when r1 is ready, and the last two follow. The core used cycles the in-order core wasted.

The rows also show something that didn't change: the **commits** (C) are still in program order. `add r4` executes in cycle 2 but commits in cycle 5, with the load and the add before it.

</details>

## How the hardware does it

An out-of-order core splits each instruction's life into steps:

1. **Fetch and decode in order**, as before.
2. **Dispatch** into a waiting area (an **issue queue**, or reservation stations), and into the **reorder buffer**, a queue in program order.
3. **Issue out of order:** each cycle, pick instructions whose inputs are ready and send them to free execution units. When one finishes, it broadcasts its result, and instructions waiting for it **wake up**.
4. **Commit in order:** the oldest instruction in the reorder buffer leaves once it has finished, writing its result to the program-visible registers.

This is **dataflow** execution: the order comes from the dependences, not from the listing. The idea goes back to Robert Tomasulo's design for the IBM System/360 Model 91 in 1967.

Why commit in order? So the program can never tell. If an instruction faults, or a branch turns out to have been mispredicted, everything after it can still be cancelled, because none of it has committed. The last lesson in this course is about exactly that.

<KeyIdea>Out of order: issue when ready, commit in program order. Independent work fills the gaps that long-latency instructions leave.</KeyIdea>

## The window

Instructions can only run ahead if the core has room to hold them while older ones are still waiting. That room is the **window**: the number of instructions in flight, which is the reorder buffer's size. Real cores hold a few hundred.

Make the load block finish in 6 cycles. You may change any setting: out of order, width and window.

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

You need all three: **out of order**, a width of **2** or more, and a window of **8** or more. Then it takes 6 cycles.

| Settings | Cycles |
|---|---|
| in order, width 1 | 9 |
| out of order, width 1 | 7 |
| in order, width 2 | 7 |
| out of order, width 2, window 32 | 6 |
| out of order, width 2, window 4 | 7 |
| out of order, width 2, window 2 | 9 |

With a window of 2, nothing can enter until the two oldest instructions (the load and the add waiting for it) have committed. So the core can't look past the load at all, and it's back to in-order speed. A window of 4 lets two more in, but not the whole block. Width and out-of-order issue are useless without a window big enough to find independent work. That's why real cores spend so much hardware on a large reorder buffer and issue queue.

</details>
