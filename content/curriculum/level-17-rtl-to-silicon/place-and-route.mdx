Synthesis gave a netlist: cells and the connections between them. Two more steps turn it into something a chip can be: **placement** decides where each cell goes, and **routing** decides which wires connect them. On an FPGA the cells and wires already exist and these steps choose which to use; on an ASIC they decide where the transistors and metal will be manufactured. The reports below are from nextpnr on an iCE40 HX1K FPGA.

## Placement and routing

- **Placement** assigns every cell to a site on the chip: here, one of 1,280 logic cells (each a 4-input lookup table, a carry bit and a flip-flop), 112 input/output pins, 16 RAM blocks and 8 global clock buffers. A good placement keeps connected cells close, because distance becomes delay.
- **Routing** connects them through the chip's wires and switches, so that every connection exists and no wire is used twice.

Both are search problems far too big to solve exactly, so tools use heuristics (nextpnr's default placer solves an analytic approximation, then refines it), then measure timing on the result. That's why the report has a frequency estimate after placement and the real figure after routing.

## When it doesn't fit

Now a 32 × 32 multiplier on the same chip. Predict what happens.

<HdlRun design="rtl/pnr-too-big" caption='A 32 x 32 multiplier on the same chip' show="mult.v" predict />

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

It needs 2,813 logic cells, **219%** of the chip, and placement stops with an error: no logic cell (BEL, basic element) is left for one of the cells. A multiplier's size grows with the square of its width, so doubling the width needs about four times the logic: 698 to 2,813 here.

The options are a bigger device; a different design, such as a multiplier that takes several cycles and reuses one smaller unit; or a chip with **DSP blocks**, dedicated multiplier hardware that many FPGAs include for exactly this reason.

</details>

## Congestion

A design that fits can still fail: the cells fit, but there aren't enough wires in one region to connect them, or the detours make paths too slow. Explain why a design at 90% utilisation is much harder to place and route than one at 50%.

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

At high utilisation the placer has little freedom: connected cells end up far apart, and the busiest regions need more wires than pass through them. That's **congestion**. The router has to detour around full channels, which makes paths longer, so timing gets worse, or it fails to route at all.

That's why designers usually aim well below 100%, often at about 70 to 80% on an FPGA. On an ASIC they leave room for routing and add buffers on long wires.

</details>

## From layout to tape-out

An FPGA flow ends with a **bitstream** that configures the existing chip; it can be changed in seconds. An ASIC flow ends with **layout**: the exact shapes of every transistor and wire on every layer, sent to a factory. After that, nothing can be fixed without making new masks, which costs a fortune. List the checks you'd want before sending it.

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

- **Static timing analysis** on the final layout, with the real wire lengths, at the slowest and fastest process corners, voltages and temperatures: every setup and hold check must pass.
- **DRC (design rule checking):** every shape obeys the factory's rules (minimum widths, spacings, overlaps, metal densities), so it can actually be made.
- **LVS (layout versus schematic):** the circuit extracted from the layout matches the netlist exactly, with no missing or extra connections.
- **Power and signal checks:** voltage drop across the power grid, current density in the wires (electromigration) and crosstalk between neighbouring wires.
- **Equivalence checking:** the final netlist still behaves like the RTL after every optimisation.

When everything passes, the design is **taped out**: the layout is sent for mask making, a name kept from the days when it went on magnetic tape. Open-source flows such as OpenLane run all of these steps (synthesis with Yosys, placement and routing, timing analysis, DRC and LVS) with open process kits such as SkyWater's 130 nm.

</details>

<KeyIdea>Placement chooses where every cell goes and routing wires them together, and their quality sets the real speed. A design must fit, route and meet timing; an ASIC must also pass DRC and LVS before tape-out, because after that nothing can be fixed.</KeyIdea>
