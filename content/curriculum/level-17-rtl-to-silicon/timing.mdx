In Foundations you saw that the longest path through a circuit sets how quickly its outputs settle. In a clocked design that becomes a hard rule with a name: **timing**. This lesson uses real reports from **nextpnr**, which places and routes designs onto a real FPGA, the Lattice iCE40 HX1K (1,280 logic cells), and then measures every path.

## Setup and hold

A flip-flop samples its input at the clock edge. For that to work reliably, the input must be steady for a short window around the edge:

- **Setup time:** the input must arrive some time *before* the edge.
- **Hold time:** it must stay unchanged some time *after* the edge.

Between two flip-flops sits combinational logic. When the first flip-flop changes, its new value travels through the logic and must reach the second one before the next edge, minus its setup time. So for every path from one flip-flop to the next:

```
clock-to-output delay + logic delay + routing delay + setup time  ≤  clock period
```

The path with the largest total is the **critical path**, and it sets the maximum clock frequency: 1 divided by its delay. A **hold** violation is the opposite problem: a path so short that the new value races through before the receiving flip-flop has finished capturing the old one. Hold problems don't depend on the clock rate, which is why they can break a chip at any speed.

## Reading a real timing report

This design multiplies two 16-bit numbers between two sets of registers. The operands come from two linear-feedback shift registers (pseudo-random sequences generated on the chip), so the design needs only a clock pin and one output pin. The whole multiply happens in one clock period, and the target is 50 MHz.

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

- **Size:** the design uses 698 of the 1,280 logic cells (54%).
- **Two frequency figures:**
  - **68.70 MHz** is an **estimate after placement**, when the cells have positions but the wires don't exist yet.
  - **66.69 MHz** is the real figure **after routing**: the actual wires add delay.
  - Both pass the 50 MHz target.
- **The critical path report** (it belongs to the routed design): 6.37 ns of logic plus 8.62 ns of routing, about 14.99 ns, and 1 / 14.99 ns is 66.7 MHz.

More than half the delay is in the **wires**. On a real chip a signal crosses switch boxes and long tracks between cells, and on modern chips routing often dominates. That's why place and route, the next lesson, matters so much for speed.

</details>

## When timing fails

The same design, with a 100 MHz target:

`FAIL at 100.00 MHz`, and nextpnr reports it as an `ERROR` and exits with an error status. The routed design still reaches only 66.69 MHz: a 100 MHz clock gives a 10 ns period, and the critical path needs about 15 ns. A chip run at 100 MHz would capture half-finished products.

To **close timing**, you either lower the clock, find a faster circuit, or cut the path into shorter pieces.

</details>

## Pipelining for speed

This version splits the multiply into two stages. Stage one computes four 8 × 8 partial products and stores them in registers. Stage two adds them up. Each stage has less logic between its registers.

<HdlRun design="rtl/timing-mult-pipe" caption='The multiplier in two pipeline stages' predict />

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

The routed clock rises from 66.69 MHz to **91.81 MHz**, and the critical path is now 4.21 + 6.68 = 10.89 ns. It uses 680 logic cells, about the same: 103 cells now hold both a LUT and a flip-flop, against 50 before, because of the extra registers. (Here the routed figure even beat the post-placement estimate of 91.02 MHz; estimates are only estimates.)

The cost is **latency**: each product now takes two clock cycles to come out, and the extra registers take area. The gain is **throughput**: a new product every faster cycle. It's the same trade-off as the five-stage processor pipeline in the Pipelining course, at the scale of one multiplier.

</details>

<KeyIdea>Every path between two flip-flops must settle within one clock period, minus setup time. The slowest one, logic plus routing, sets the maximum frequency. Pipelining shortens paths, trading latency and registers for clock rate.</KeyIdea>
