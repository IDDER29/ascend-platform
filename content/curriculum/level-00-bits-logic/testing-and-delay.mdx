So far every circuit in this course has been correct the moment its inputs changed, and checked by filling in its truth table. Real circuits raise two questions. How do you test something too big to check by hand? And how long does a change take to get through the gates?

By the end you'll be able to say when exhaustive testing is possible, explain propagation delay and the critical path, and see why speed costs gates.

## Test every row, while you can

A circuit with n input bits has 2 to the power n possible inputs, one truth-table row each. A half adder has 2 inputs and 4 rows; a full adder has 3 inputs and 8 rows. Checking every row, called <b>exhaustive testing</b>, proves the circuit right.

The rows double with every input bit, though:

<TruthTable caption='Rows in an exhaustive test' rowsJson='[["circuit", "input bits", "rows"], ["half adder", "2", "4"], ["full adder", "3", "8"], ["4-bit adder", "8", "256"], ["8-bit adder", "16", "65,536"], ["8-bit ALU with 8 operations", "16 plus 3 for the operation", "524,288"]]' />

At one row a second, the 8-bit ALU would take about six days without a break. So engineers do two things. They test each small part exhaustively, by computer, and trust that correct parts wired correctly make a correct whole. And they test the big circuit with chosen <b>test vectors</b>: inputs picked to hit the edges, like 0, 255, 127 + 1, and A equal to B.

## Gates take time

A gate's output doesn't change the instant its inputs do. Transistors take a moment to switch, so each gate adds a small <b>propagation delay</b>, a tiny fraction of a nanosecond on a modern chip. A signal passing through ten gates in a row arrives after ten delays.

While a change is still travelling through the gates, an output can briefly show a wrong value before it settles. A circuit's output is only trustworthy once every path through it has settled.

## The critical path

The <b>critical path</b> is the longest chain of gates from any input to any output. It decides when the outputs are ready.

In a ripple-carry adder, the critical path is the carry. Each full adder's carry out depends on its carry in, which depends on the column to its right, and so on. With about 2 gate delays per column for the carry:

<TruthTable caption='Ripple carry: roughly 2 gate delays per column' rowsJson='[["adder width", "gate delays for the carry to ripple through"], ["8 bits", "about 16"], ["32 bits", "about 64"], ["64 bits", "about 128"]]' />

Doubling the width doubles the wait. A <b>carry-lookahead adder</b> avoids that by working out the carries in parallel with extra gates, so its delay grows much more slowly. It is faster, and it is bigger. That is the usual trade: more gates for less time.

<KeyIdea>The slowest path through a circuit sets its speed. You can often go faster by spending more gates.</KeyIdea>

## Why this sets the clock

In the next course you'll meet the clock, which tells registers when to store a new value. A register must only store an ALU's result once it has settled, so the time between clock ticks can't be shorter than the circuit's critical path. That is a big part of why every chip has a maximum clock speed, and why shortening the critical path is one of a chip designer's main jobs.
