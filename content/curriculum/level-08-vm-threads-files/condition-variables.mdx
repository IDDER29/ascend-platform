A mutex stops threads from touching shared data at the same time. Threads often also need to **wait** for each other: a consumer waits until there's something to take, a producer until there's room. This lesson is about waiting well.

## Waiting by spinning

The simplest way to wait is to check a flag over and over until it changes. That's **busy-waiting**. Here one thread busy-waits and another waits on a condition variable. Both wait for the same half second, and each reports how much CPU time it used. The numbers are real and vary a little between runs.

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

The busy waiter used about 500 ms of CPU: a whole core, flat out, for the entire wait, doing nothing useful. The thread waiting on a condition variable used about 0.1 ms. It was asleep, off the CPU, until it was signalled. On a busy machine, the spinning core is a core other threads couldn't use.

</details>

## Sleep until something changes

A **condition variable** is a place where threads sleep until another thread signals that something changed. It always works with a mutex that protects the shared data:

```c
pthread_mutex_lock(&lock);
while (count == 0) {                  /* nothing to take yet */
    pthread_cond_wait(&not_empty, &lock);
}
/* count > 0 here, and we hold the lock */
```

`pthread_cond_wait` **releases the mutex and sleeps, as one step**, so a signal can't slip in between the check and the sleep. When it wakes, it takes the mutex back before returning. On the other side, a thread that changes the data does so under the same mutex, then calls `pthread_cond_signal` (wake one waiter) or `pthread_cond_broadcast` (wake all of them).

## A bounded queue

Here's the classic **producer-consumer** queue: a ring buffer that holds at most 4 items, one mutex, and two condition variables. `not_full` is for producers waiting for room, and `not_empty` for consumers waiting for an item. A producer puts in the numbers 1 to 20 while a consumer adds up everything it takes. Predict the two lines.

<CRun program="vm-threads-files/th-cv-queue" caption='A bounded queue of 4' predict />

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

All 20 items are consumed, they sum to 210, and the queue ends empty. On every run, even though the two threads interleave differently each time. The producer often finds the queue full and sleeps on `not_full`. The consumer often finds it empty and sleeps on `not_empty`. Each wakes the other after changing `count`.

Each side signals the *other* side's condition: `put` signals `not_empty` and `take` signals `not_full`. Every change to `buffer`, `head`, `tail` and `count` happens with the mutex held.

</details>

## Why the while loop

Here two consumers wait for an item. Main adds **one** item but wakes **both** with `pthread_cond_broadcast`. The consumers use `if` instead of `while` to decide whether to wait. Predict what they report.

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

One consumer took the item, and the other **woke up to an empty queue**. Both were woken. The first to get the mutex took the item. The second, having checked only once with `if`, carried on as if there were an item. In a real queue it would have read garbage from the buffer and made `count` negative.

The second consumer checks again, finds nothing, and goes back to sleep until a second item arrives.

There's a second reason for `while`: POSIX allows **spurious wakeups**, where a wait returns with no signal at all. So the rule has no exceptions: always wait in a loop that re-checks the condition.

</details>

<KeyIdea>Lock the mutex, wait in a while loop on the condition, act, unlock. Signal after changing what someone may be waiting for, with the same mutex held.</KeyIdea>
