Guessing "not taken" was free when a branch fell through, but a loop branch is taken almost every time. A **branch predictor** does better by remembering what each branch did before, and guessing that it will do it again.

## A static guess

A loop of 10 iterations, run 10 times, has a branch that goes: taken 9 times, then not taken once to exit, over and over. The lab below shows each prediction, what really happened, and whether the guess was right. Try "always not taken" and "always taken".

"Always not taken" is right 10 times out of 100: only the exits. "Always taken" is right 90 times. For loops, "taken" is the better static guess, which is why some processors predict backward branches (to an earlier address, like a loop's) as taken and forward ones as not taken.

## Remember what happened

Static guesses treat every branch the same. A **dynamic** predictor keeps a small table, indexed by bits of the branch's address, and learns per branch:

- **1-bit:** each entry remembers the last outcome, and predicts it again.
- **2-bit saturating counter:** each entry counts from 0 to 3. 0 and 1 predict not taken, 2 and 3 predict taken. A taken branch adds 1 and a not-taken one subtracts 1, stopping at 0 and 3. One surprise moves the counter one step, but not across the middle if it was at 0 or 3.

Switch the lab above to "1-bit", then to "2-bit", and look at where the wrong guesses are.

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

- **1-bit: 80 of 100.** It's wrong twice per loop run: at the exit (it remembered "taken") and at the next run's first test (it remembered the exit's "not taken").
- **2-bit: 89 of 100.** After the first run it sits at "strongly taken". The exit drops it only to "weakly taken", which still predicts taken, so the next run's first test is right. It's wrong only at the 10 exits, plus once at the very start, while it warms up from weakly not taken.

That one extra bit of memory makes a predictor tolerate a single surprise.

</details>

## Learning patterns with history

This branch alternates: taken, not taken, taken, not taken. A counter can't learn that, because the last outcome is always the wrong guess for the next. **gshare** keeps a **global history register**: the outcomes of the last few branches, as bits. It combines that history with the branch's address (with XOR) to pick a counter, so "after a taken" and "after a not taken" use different counters. Reach 90%.

<PredictorLab caption='An alternating branch' kindJson='"2-bit"' kindsJson='["2-bit","gshare"]' patternJson='{"pc":128,"pattern":"TN","repeat":50}' goalJson='{"minAccuracy":0.9}' />

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

The 2-bit counter gets **0 of 100**. It starts at weakly not taken and sees a taken branch, so it moves to weakly taken just in time to see a not taken, and moves back. It's wrong every single time.

gshare with 4 bits of history gets **97 of 100**. Once the history is filled in, "last outcome was taken" and "last outcome was not taken" select different counters, and each learns its own answer. The 3 misses are while it warms up. Global history also lets one branch's outcome predict another's, as in `if (x > 0) ...; if (x > 5) ...`, where the first branch tells you a lot about the second.

</details>

<KeyIdea>A predictor bets that a branch will behave as it did before: a 2-bit counter tolerates one surprise, and history lets it learn patterns and links between branches.</KeyIdea>

## What a wrong guess costs

In this course's five-stage pipeline, a wrong guess flushes 2 instructions. Real CPUs resolve branches much deeper, after a dozen or more stages, so a misprediction typically costs around 15 to 20 cycles. The average cost is:

CPI = 1 + (branches per instruction) × (miss rate) × (penalty)

If 20% of instructions are branches and 5% of those are mispredicted, what's the CPI with a 2-cycle penalty, and with a 15-cycle one?

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

- 2-cycle penalty: 1 + 0.2 × 0.05 × 2 = **1.02**.
- 15-cycle penalty: 1 + 0.2 × 0.05 × 15 = **1.15**.

The deeper the pipeline, the more each miss costs, so deep pipelines need very accurate predictors. Modern ones combine many tables and long histories and are often right well over 95% of the time on typical code. Predicting the direction isn't enough, either: a taken branch also needs its target address before decode, which comes from a **branch target buffer**, a cache of recent branches' targets.

</details>
