The last lesson ended with a D flip-flop: one bit that changes only when the clock ticks. This lesson is about the two halves of that sentence. First the <b>clock</b>, the signal that says "now". Then the <b>register</b>, a row of flip-flops that stores a whole number at once.

By the end you'll be able to say what a clock tick is, what a gigahertz counts, how a register is built, and why it needs a switch that says when to take in a new value.

## The clock: a heartbeat for the chip

A <b>clock</b> is a wire whose voltage flips between low and high, over and over, at a steady rate: a square wave. Every flip-flop in the chip is connected to it. The moment the wave rises from 0 to 1, the <b>rising edge</b>, is a tick.

Between ticks, gates work things out: an adder's output ripples into place, signals settle. At each tick, flip-flops capture the settled results. Then the next round of working-out begins. The clock is what keeps thousands of parts in step.

<Visual type='diagram' caption='A clock signal: work between ticks, capture at each tick' description='A square wave alternating low and high at even spacing. Each rising edge is marked with a small upward arrow labeled tick. The flat stretches between ticks are shaded and labeled "gates settle", and each tick is labeled "flip-flops capture".' />

How fast can it tick? Only as fast as the slowest path of gates can settle. A clock rate of 3 GHz means 3 billion ticks every second, roughly one third of a billionth of a second per tick.

<KeyIdea>The clock doesn't compute anything. It decides when results are captured, so every part of the chip moves forward together.</KeyIdea>

## A register: flip-flops in a row

Put eight D flip-flops side by side and wire all their clock inputs to the same clock. Give each one its own data wire, D0 to D7. At a tick, all eight copy their inputs at once: you've stored a byte. That row is a <b>register</b>.

Widen it to 32 or 64 flip-flops and you have the registers of a real processor. The program counter and the instruction register from the fetch-execute loop are exactly this: rows of flip-flops on the clock.

<Visual type='diagram' caption='An 8-bit register' description='Eight D flip-flop boxes in a row. Each has its own input arrow on top, labeled D7 down to D0, and its own output arrow below, labeled Q7 to Q0. One shared line labeled CLK runs along the bottom and connects to every box.' />

## When to write: the load input

There's a catch. If every register copied its inputs on every tick, nothing could be kept longer than one tick; the next tick would overwrite it with whatever was on the wires.

So a register gets one more input: <b>load</b> (also called write-enable). When load is 1, the tick copies the new value in. When load is 0, the tick passes and the register keeps what it has. Inside, each bit has a small chooser in front of its flip-flop: on a tick it either takes the new bit or feeds its own old bit back in.

<TruthTable caption='A register at each tick' rowsJson='[["load", "at the tick"], ["0", "keeps its value"], ["1", "takes the new value"]]' />

<KeyIdea>The clock says when anything may change; the load input says which register actually does.</KeyIdea>

## From one register to many

A processor has a handful of registers. A computer also needs room for millions of values, and it can't wire each one up by hand. The next lesson stacks registers into rows with numbers, <b>addresses</b>, so any one of them can be picked out: that's memory.
