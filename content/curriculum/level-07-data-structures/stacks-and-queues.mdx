Some structures are defined less by how they store data than by the **order** they give it back. A **stack** returns the last item added first: last in, first out (LIFO), like a pile of plates. A **queue** returns the first item added first: first in, first out (FIFO), like a line at a till. You've met a stack already: the call stack, where the most recent call returns first.

## A stack checks brackets

A stack needs only two operations, **push** onto the top and **pop** from the top, and both are O(1). On a growing array the top is simply `items[len - 1]`:

```c
static int pop(Stack *s, char *out) {
    if (s->len == 0) {
        return -1;              /* empty: report it, never read items[-1] */
    }
    *out = s->items[--s->len];
    return 0;
}
```

This program checks brackets with a stack: every `(`, `[` or `{` is pushed, and every closing bracket pops and must match. Predict which of the five input lines are balanced.

<CRun program="data-structures/sq-brackets" caption='Checking brackets with a stack' predict />

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

- `a[i] = f(x) + {1}`: balanced.
- `if (a[0] > (b)) {`: **not** balanced. The `{` is pushed and never closed, so the stack isn't empty at the end.
- `(]`: not balanced. `]` pops `(`, which isn't its match.
- `)(`: not balanced. `)` tries to pop an empty stack, and `pop` reports that instead of reading outside the array.
- `{[()()]}`: balanced. The nesting unwinds in exactly reverse order.

Compilers check your brackets the same way. The stack owns its array, and `balanced` frees it before returning, whichever way the check ends.

</details>

## A queue in a ring

A queue adds at the **tail** and removes from the **head**. On a plain array, removing from the front would mean shifting every item down: O(n). A **ring buffer** avoids that. It keeps the head's index and the item count, and wraps around with `%`: the tail slot is `(head + count) % SLOTS`, and dequeue moves `head` forward one slot. Both operations are O(1), and nothing is ever shifted.

This queue has 4 slots. Follow the slot numbers as items come and go, and predict where 500 and 600 go and what happens to 700.

<CRun program="data-structures/sq-queue" caption='A ring buffer of 4 slots' predict />

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

100, 200 and 300 fill slots 0 to 2, and dequeueing two items moves the head to slot 2. 400 takes slot 3, then the tail **wraps**: 500 goes to slot 0 and 600 to slot 1, the slots freed by the dequeues. Now all 4 slots are in use, so enqueueing 700 reports "full". Dequeueing then returns 300, 400, 500 and 600, in the order they arrived, from slots 2, 3, 0 and 1.

Keeping a `count`, rather than just head and tail, is what tells "full" apart from "empty": in both, the head and tail point at the same slot.

</details>

<KeyIdea>A stack gives back the newest item, a queue the oldest. On an array, both run in O(1): the stack works at the end, and the queue wraps around a ring.</KeyIdea>

## Popping an empty stack

This stack's `pop` trusts its caller. One item is pushed and two are popped. It's built with AddressSanitizer: predict what the second pop does.

<CRun program="data-structures/sq-pop-empty" caption='A pop that trusts its caller' predict />

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

The first pop returns 7. The second runs `--s->len`, making `len` -1, and reads `items[-1]`, 4 bytes **before** the heap block. ASan reports a **heap-buffer-overflow** in `pop` at `main.c:10`. Without ASan it would quietly return whatever bytes sit there, often part of malloc's own bookkeeping.

The fix is the check from the first section: `pop` returns -1 for an empty stack and gives the value through a pointer, so the caller has to look at the result. A structure should guard its own invariants rather than hope every caller will.

</details>
